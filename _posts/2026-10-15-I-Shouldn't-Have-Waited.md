I was first made aware of Tom Sweterlitsch's "The Gone World" a number of years after it was published, in the fall of 2024. I immediately disregarded it. The summary from the back of the book did absolutely nothing for me at the time.

From the publisher:
>Inception meets True Detective in this science fiction thriller of spellbinding tension and staggering scope that follows a special agent into a savage murder case with grave implications for the fate of mankind...

> Shannon Moss is part of a clandestine division within the Naval Criminal Investigative Service. In western Pennsylvania, 1997, she is assigned to solve the murder of a Navy SEAL's family--and to locate his vanished teenage daughter. Though she can't share the information with conventional law enforcement, Moss discovers that the missing SEAL was an astronaut aboard the spaceship U.S.S. Libra—a ship assumed lost to the currents of Deep Time. Moss knows first-hand the mental trauma of time-travel and believes the SEAL's experience with the future has triggered this violence.

>Determined to find the missing girl and driven by a troubling connection from her own past, Moss travels ahead in time to explore possible versions of the future, seeking evidence to crack the present-day case. To
her horror, the future reveals that it's not only the fate of a family that hinges on her work, for what she witnesses rising over time's horizon and hurtling toward the present is the Terminus: the terrifying and cataclysmic end of humanity itself.

>Luminous and unsettling, The Gone World bristles with world-shattering ideas yet remains at its heart an intensely human story.

For as much as I love good sci-fi, for as much as I love good cosmic horror, for as much as I think Season One of "True Detective" is the peak of television, I couldn't get into the idea of this book. I had it in my head that this was a run of the mill police procedural, focused on the workings of the NCIS. No matter how many people told me to read the book, I refused. I judged the book, not by its cover, but close enough.

I cannot stress enough how wrong I was about this book. "The Gone World" is the best book I have read this year, far surpassing fellow five star read "Exhalations", which should say much more about "The Gone World" than it does "Exhalations", which I still consider excellent. "The Gone World" rips. I have to think it will not be surpassed any time soon.

 The logical question is: What makes this book worthy of such praise? I have three answer to this question.

 1. Consistently good prose. This is the easy, boring answer. Sweterlitsch is clear and concise. The imagery is top notch, the characters pop off of the page. I can think of literally one sentence in the entire novel that I would change, if given the chance. "[He] put a gun to my head and I felt pain" was such a jarring sentence that it pulled me out. But it is the only example of even below average prose in the entire book. In most books, even books I love, I can pick out passages that pull me out of the experience. That "The Gone World" has but one such sentence is incredible. This is a technically well-written book that doesn't sacrifice anything for that level of precision.
 2. A banger of an opener. The opening chapter of this book is the most compelling opening to any book that I have ever read. It introduces the main character, her struggle, the overarching plot device, and a ton of scientific jargon that doesn't bog down the story, but enhances it. I contend that if you read the first chapter of this book and you are not compelled, you did not pay attention. If this chapter existed as a standalone short story, it would still be talked about and held in high regard as a classic (with maybe a little extra added to flesh it out).
 3. Tom Sweterlitsch understands cosmic horror in a way that I don't think many modern authors do. His command of the core ideas of cosmic horror: human insignificance, cosmic indifference, the fragile sanity of man to name a few is unmatched. Authors such as Langan and VanderMeer are incredible authors of cosmic horror, but they don't cut quite as deeply or quite as efficiently as Sweterlitsch. There are passages that I read and had to put the book down for hours to try to come to terms with the consequences of what I had read. It's simply a masterclass in cosmic horror.

The book is complicated - there are more than a few diagrams floating around the internet that you can consult to make sense of the time travel in the story, though I think that you should do your best to avoid them until after you've given the book a shot and made it through at least once. I personally find it best to avoid spoilers at any cost, save for what's on the back of the book. But you do you.

I hope that you'll give "The Gone World" a shot. I hope that you'll be as compelled by the opening as I was. I hope that you'll reach the same points I did and have to put the book down because you just have to try to parse what you just read before moving on. I hope, like me, you might not quite be able to.
